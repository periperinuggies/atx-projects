Horizon 1 Deep Dive

Start: Modular co-location
on Pilbara mining surplus.

Why modular first?

  • Containerised data centres deploy in 12–18 months vs 4–6 years for permanent builds
  • Lower capital risk — prove demand before committing $200M+
  • Relocatable — if one mining partnership fails, hardware moves
  • Australian Defence already uses containerised DCs at remote sites (Bird & Bird, 2025)
  • Modular cost premium (~20–30% per MW) is offset by speed-to-market and optionality

The harsh environment concern — addressed

Pilbara temperatures: 40–48°C in summer. Humidity: low. Dust: high. Standard air-cooling fails.

Solution: Dielectric fluid immersion cooling. The GPU clusters are submerged in non-conductive fluid — no fans, no air intake, no dust ingestion. Optimised for exactly this environment. Mitsubishi Electric and others offer containerised solutions rated for extreme environments.

Mining companies already run sensitive electronics in this environment. It's a solved problem.

Horizon 2 Deep Dive

The permanent campus:
Onslow, Pilbara coast.

Why Onslow?

  • Seawater cooling: deep-water port means unlimited, low-cost thermal sink — eliminates the single biggest opex item for hot-climate DCs
  • Solar irradiance: among the highest in the world (~2,500+ kWh/m²/year). Solar LCOE here is among the cheapest on earth
  • Existing infrastructure: Onslow has a functioning port, airport, and was a WWII submarine base — civil works cheaper than true greenfield
  • Land: WA Crown land allocation; near-zero acquisition cost
  • Wind: consistent trade winds year-round for wind generation complement
  • Cable: DJSC system connectivity into Pilbara corridor to Singapore (40Tbps)
Seawater Cooling — The Moat

Water cooling reduces Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) to 1.1–1.2 vs 1.4–1.6 for air-cooled DCs. At 400 MW, that's 80–200 MW of power not wasted on cooling. At A$25–35/MWh, that's A$17–61M/year in saved energy costs vs an air-cooled competitor. Seawater takes it further — no freshwater licence, no drought risk, no cooling tower capex.

Power Risk — Deep Dive

"What if the mine needs
its power back?"

The single question every investor will ask. Here’s how we’ve structured it away.

The risk, clearly stated

Mining operations have variable power demand. A production surge, equipment failure, or renewable shortfall could cause a mining partner to reclaim power allocated to Red Earth Compute — interrupting customer workloads and potentially triggering SLA penalties or contract liability.

This is a real risk. We don’t pretend otherwise. But it’s a structurally managed risk, not an existential one.

Five-layer mitigation stack

1
Contractual floor
PPA includes a minimum guaranteed non-curtailable allocation (e.g. 20 MW firm). Mining co can only reclaim power above that floor.
2
72-hour notice clause
Any curtailment above the floor requires 72-hour minimum notice — time to gracefully checkpoint running jobs and notify customers.
3
On-site BESS buffer
2–4 hrs battery storage per site absorbs short outages without customer impact. ~40 MWh at 10 MW — ~A$20–30M, already standard at mining sites.
4
Interruptible SLA tiers
Firm capacity — premium price, backed by BESS + curtailment floor.  Interruptible capacity — discounted, for AI training jobs that checkpoint and resume natively. Training workloads are inherently interruption-tolerant.
5
Multi-site load balancing
H1 targets 3+ simultaneous sites. If one partner reduces availability, active jobs shift to another. No single point of failure.
Mine closure risk — the longer view

If a mine winds down operations, does the power disappear entirely?

Structural answer: We structure PPAs against the renewable asset (the solar farm / wind farm), not the mine’s operating status. A solar farm built by Fortescue keeps generating whether or not the mine is running. The PPA is tied to the generation asset, not to the mine’s production volume.

For Fortescue’s 690 MW solar farm specifically: that asset has a 25–30-year design life and will outlast any individual mine. Our contract runs with the asset.

Connectivity — honest note

H1 Pilbara sites rely on microwave backhaul to the Vocus/Telstra fibre ring — adequate for batch AI training workloads (not latency-sensitive), but bandwidth-limited for inference or real-time applications. This is by design: H1 customers are training-only.

The DJSC Pilbara–Singapore spur is not yet built. A fibre spur to an Onslow landing point is a H2 capital commitment (~A$80–120M) and is included in the Series A deployment plan. H1 connectivity is sufficient for the target workload; H2 removes the constraint entirely.

Why immersion, not air cooling?

Standard air-cooled data centres fail in Pilbara conditions: 48°C ambient, red dust, and humidity cycles. Immersion cooling eliminates every one of those failure modes. GPUs submerged in dielectric fluid have no fans, no air intakes, no filters to clog. The fluid is chemically inert and non-conductive. This isn’t a compromise for remote deployment — it’s a performance upgrade: immersion-cooled H100s run cooler, faster, and with better reliability than air-cooled equivalents.
