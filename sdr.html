🗼 Commercial Antenna System — Warehouse Installation

A tall warehouse installation changes the game entirely. Height is the single biggest factor in SDR performance — every metre of mast extends your line-of-sight horizon. Here's a commercial-grade stack designed around your setup.

Core Principle: Signal quality is won at the antenna, not at the radio.
The BladeRF and HackRF are only as good as what you feed them. A mediocre antenna + long cheap coax + no LNA will completely negate the quality of either radio. The rule: put amplification at the antenna, use quality coax, and filter aggressively. Everything else is secondary.

⛓ Signal Chain — How It All Connects

Recommended Signal Chain (per antenna port)
Antenna (top of mast) → Mast-head LNA + filter → Lightning arrestor → LMR-400 coax (long run) → Weatherproof entry gland → Coax splitter (if multi-radio) → BladeRF / HackRF → Raspberry Pi / VM
Mast-Head LNA (Critical)
The LNA must be at the antenna, not at the radio. Every metre of coax before the LNA adds noise — amplifying after the cable amplifies the cable noise too. Recommended: Uputronics Filtered Preamp (band-specific) or RTL-SDR Wideband LNA. Uputronics units include a SAW filter that kills out-of-band interference before it reaches the radio.
~AUD $40–90 · Position: immediately at antenna
Lightning Arrestor (Non-Negotiable)
A tall warehouse antenna on a metal mast is a lightning conductor. A coaxial gas discharge lightning arrestor inline between antenna and entry point protects everything downstream. Proxicast ANT-234 or equivalent N-type/SMA inline arrestor. Ground the arrestor and mast bonding to earth. Without this you will lose expensive hardware in the first storm.
~AUD $30–60 · Position: top of coax run, before building entry
LMR-400 Coax
For any run over 5m, use LMR-400 or equivalent low-loss coax. Standard RG-58 loses ~6 dB per 30m at 1 GHz — you'd be throwing away most of your signal. LMR-400 loses ~2.5 dB per 30m. For a 15m mast run, the difference is 2 dB — that's real aircraft and real range. Use N-type connectors throughout; SMA adaptor at the radio end only.
~AUD $6–8/m · Use N-type throughout, SMA adaptor at radio only
4-Port Coax Splitter
Run one antenna feed into a powered splitter to feed BladeRF + HackRF + dedicated ADS-B receiver simultaneously. A passive splitter loses 6 dB (half power to each port) — an active distribution amplifier maintains signal level. For 2 radios a passive is fine; for 3–4 use a Mini-Circuits or similar active splitter.
~AUD $30–150 passive/active · Position: indoors, after coax entry

📡 Hardware: Pick Your Weapon

The BladeRF and HackRF are both serious tools but fundamentally different in philosophy. The right choice depends on what you're building.

Full duplex is the critical differentiator — it lets you receive and transmit simultaneously, enabling MITM attacks, active cellular research, and protocol relay. The FPGA also lets you offload signal processing, enabling real-time work that software alone can't do. The narrower frequency range is the only real limitation.

The Swiss Army knife of SDR — huge frequency range, open hardware, massive community. Excellent for passive intelligence and wide-spectrum sweeps. Half-duplex limits active research but it covers 5 GHz WiFi, 2.4 GHz, and anything up to 6 GHz that the BladeRF misses. Both together is the real answer.
Verdict: Start with the BladeRF x40 if you're serious about active research — full duplex + reprogrammable FPGA is a ceiling that HackRF can never hit. Add HackRF later for the wider frequency sweep. Together they cover the full spectrum from 1 MHz to 6 GHz with no gaps.

⚠ Australian Legal Boundaries

  • Receiving most signals is legal in Australia — interception of private communications is not (Telecommunications (Interception and Access) Act 1979)
  • Transmitting requires an ACMA licence unless within your own property on permitted bands — unlicensed transmission can attract significant fines
  • IMSI catchers (fake base stations) are illegal to operate outside of law enforcement / controlled lab environments
  • GPS spoofing is a federal offence regardless of context
  • GSM/LTE passive monitoring of control channels is a grey area — ACMA has not prosecuted academic researchers but the law is ambiguous
  • Safe zone: passive receive, own property TX experiments, isolated RF cage testing, academic research with documented consent

Council / Planning — City of Stirling
Industrial zoning is the most forgiving classification for antenna structures in WA. Under City of Stirling Local Planning Scheme No. 3, structures ancillary to the primary use of an industrial lot are generally exempt from planning approval if they don't create a public safety hazard or exceed building height limits.

Practical rules:
• Masts up to ~6m above the building roofline are typically exempt from planning approval in industrial zones
• Masts exceeding ~10m above natural ground level or protruding significantly above the roof generally require a building permit for structural certification — this is a standard structural engineer sign-off, not a planning refusal
• No residential amenity concerns — industrial neighbours won't care
• A building permit is straightforward here: engage a structural engineer, provide wind loading calcs, submit to the City of Stirling. Usually ~4–6 weeks and a few hundred dollars

Bottom line: 8–10m mast is achievable with a building permit. Planning approval risk is very low.

Council / Planning — City of Nedlands
Residential zoning under City of Nedlands Local Planning Scheme No. 3 is considerably more restrictive than industrial. Nedlands is an established, heritage-conscious LGA — they take visual amenity seriously.

Practical rules:
• A small rooftop antenna (similar to a TV aerial) is generally exempt from planning approval if it doesn't project more than 2m above the roof ridge — this covers an RTL-SDR dongle on a short pole, not a serious mast
• Any mast visible from the street or neighbouring properties that materially changes the visual character of the property will likely need planning approval from Nedlands. Their scheme prioritises streetscape and character
• A 4–6m pole fixed to the rear of a dwelling (not visible from street) with a modest antenna is the practical maximum you'll get through without a planning fight
• Anything taller or more prominent: you'd need to demonstrate it doesn't adversely affect neighbours' outlook or the streetscape — outcome is uncertain and neighbour-dependent
• City of Nedlands planning counter will give you a pre-lodgement meeting for free — worth doing before you buy any hardware for this site

Bottom line: 4–6m max without planning risk. A discreet rear-of-property mount is achievable; a full commercial mast is not.

📡 The Elephant in the Room — Campbell Barracks
UNFILTERED

Campbell Barracks is the home of the Australian Special Air Service Regiment — the SAS. It's also sitting on a very interesting RF footprint. Here's what's actually known, and some honest thoughts on proximity.

What's Known
  • → The large log-periodic antenna arrays visible at Campbell Barracks are operated by 152 Signal Squadron, the Army's specialist communications unit embedded with SASR
  • → The log-periodics are HF antenna systems (roughly 3–30 MHz), used for long-range encrypted voice and data communications — the kind that bounces off the ionosphere and reaches anywhere on Earth
  • → Perth's scanner community (WARSUG) has documented these antennas publicly — they were originally sited on a hill north of the firing range and relocated to the barracks when the land was repurposed for public open space. They're not secret in the sense of being unknown; their content is classified.
  • → SASR also uses tactical VHF/UHF for local comms (encrypted), satellite terminals (SATCOM), and likely some UHF MILSATCOM. The big log-periodics are specifically the HF long-haul links.
  • → The ADF also historically coordinated with the former Gnangara VIP HF station — logs from that installation ended up at Swanbourne when Gnangara was decommissioned
Honest Assessment
  • ! Receiving HF in the 3–30 MHz range from a civilian address near a military installation is entirely legal. Passive receive is not interception under Australian law; the Radiocommunications Act 1992 prohibits disclosing or using intercepted communications, not the act of receiving. You are legally allowed to tune to any frequency. You are not allowed to decode, record, or act on encrypted military traffic.
  • ! Proximity to military infrastructure does not create legal risk for passive RF monitoring of civilian bands. Your AIS, ADS-B, weather satellite, and spectrum scanning activities are completely unrelated to military comms.
  • ! The practical interest: from a Swanbourne node, you would almost certainly see HF carrier activity from 152 Sqn systems just by nature of proximity. Log-periodics are powerful transmitters and you'd be within 500m. They'd light up a wideband HF scan like a lighthouse. You're not decoding anything — but you'd know when they're active.
  • ! The grey area: deliberately pointing equipment at a military facility with the intent to characterise its transmissions would attract ASIO attention even if technically legal. Passively scanning all HF bands and noticing military signals as part of broader spectrum work is a completely different thing. Don't make it the point of the exercise.
  • ! The non-obvious advantage: military sites are typically surrounded by RF-quiet buffer zones. No industrial equipment, no noisy switching power supplies, minimal consumer electronics interference. Campbell Barracks' perimeter may actually provide better RF quiet than surrounding suburbs, making it a surprisingly clean location for sensitive receive work.

The more interesting point is that proximity to the base may give you a quieter RF environment than you'd expect, and a clear ocean horizon that makes Swanbourne excellent for AIS and ADS-B regardless of what's next door.
